\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Industrial visual inspection asks a narrow question with unforgiving constraints: given a
handful of defect-free images of one product, decide whether a newly captured image contains
a defect, and if possible, where. The constraint that makes the problem research-worthy is
the scarcity of the evidence. Collecting defective samples of a rare failure mode is
expensive by definition -- defects are, precisely, the events that almost never happen -- and
a production line re-tooled for a new product cannot wait for a labelled corpus. Few-shot
anomaly detection therefore inverts the usual deep-learning recipe: instead of fitting a
model to many labelled examples of a fixed task, it asks how far a \emph{frozen}
representation, learned elsewhere and once, can be pressed into service for a product it has
never seen.

Two families of answers exist. The first keeps the problem purely visual: model ``normal
appearance'' from the few reference images and flag deviations from it, a line that runs from
handcrafted template comparison through statistical patch models to modern self-supervised
features~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec,defard2021padim,roth2022patchcore,oquab2024dinov2,damm2025anomalydino}.
The second brings language in: vision--language models let an inspector describe what
``defect-free'' means in words and transfer that description across
products~\cite{radford2021clip,jeong2023winclip,zhou2024anomalyclip}. Between them lies an
open empirical question -- which kind of frozen evidence, visual or linguistic, actually
carries the information that separates a subtle scratch from a shift in pose or lighting --
and a systems question of whether the two can be combined into something better than either
alone.

This report treats that junction as a controlled measurement problem. Our Part~2 reproduces
the strongest training-free visual recipe, AnomalyDINO~\cite{damm2025anomalydino}, on the six
object-centric categories of VisA~\cite{zou2022spot} and verifies the reproduction
cell-by-cell against the official pipeline. Part~3 then changes one variable at a time:
(\textbf{E1}) the visual encoder, swapping DINOv2 for CLIP's vision tower under an identical
scoring rule; (\textbf{E2}) a direct multimodal inspector (Qwen-VL) that sees the references
and the test image; (\textbf{E3/E4}) calibrated score-level fusion of the branches; and
(\textbf{E5}) the same fusion across the two visual backbones -- all under a protocol frozen
before any variant was scored, with an explicit account of latency, memory and reliability.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a verified reproduction of AnomalyDINO on VisA (all 72 protocol cells agree with the
        official pipeline to the last printed digit), together with the two harness defects
        we found and fixed on the way (cache-key collisions and a lossy float16 cache);
  \item a controlled encoder swap showing that language alignment costs 5.6 image-AUROC
        points at 1-shot in dense patch matching, does not close with more references, and
        reverses on exactly one category -- with the mechanism measured at the patch level
        (feature anisotropy) rather than asserted;
  \item an image-level error taxonomy of the two arms (63.2\% verdict agreement; 58 images
        only CLIP solves), and a pre-registered score-fusion protocol that gains $+0.68$
        points over the better arm across nine (shot, seed) cells while exposing a
        calibration gap (nominal 5\% false alarm, realised 9.4--21.4\%);
  \item \bsec{E2/E3/E4/E5 multimodal results} a multimodal branch inspected image-by-image
        against the visual arms, and a fusion result with a fixed weight, full sensitivity
        curve and honest realised false-alarm rate.
\end{itemize}
