\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked how far a frozen encoder carries few-shot industrial anomaly detection, and
measured the answer. A verified AnomalyDINO reproduction anchors the visual route at 94.61
image AUROC on VisA's six object-centric categories at 1-shot; a controlled encoder swap
shows that CLIP's language-aligned vision tower gives up 5.6 points in dense patch matching
-- a property of the features, not of sample size, traced to measurable anisotropy; the
taxonomy shows the arms disagree on 36.8\% of images, enough room for the pre-registered
fusion to gain $+0.68$ points over the better arm across nine (shot, seed) cells while
making the result more stable, at calibration gaps we report as achieved operating points
rather than nominal guarantees. The multimodal branch completes the
picture: frozen forced-choice scoring puts Qwen2-VL above both dense arms on the same
held-out images, its errors overlapping the visual arms' far less than the arms' overlap
each other -- though its fusion with DINOv2 remains post-hoc, not a confirmed result.

The wider lesson is methodological: in a regime with no training signal, every claim reduces
to either the representation or the decision rule, so both should be measured under a
protocol frozen before scoring, with per-image evidence for every aggregate number and
reliability (threshold behaviour, latency, memory) next to accuracy. Benchmarks are no
longer the bottleneck~\cite{hecklerkram2026mvtec2}; representations and decisions are.
